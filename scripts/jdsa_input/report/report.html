Disparity In, Wobble Out

What the joint depth–scale adjustment can and cannot hear from a depth prior, measured from the raw dumps — why the served depth that tracks best on KITTI is the one furthest from the lidar, and how far the ratio that gets it there can be computed instead of swept.

Scenes kitti_00 f0–1000, rellis_00000 Arms re-measured 311 Depth dumps 8 GT velodyne + odometry poses Date 2026-09-01

The interfaceWhat JDSA actually receives

One residual, per pixel, in disparity: rd = disps − s(x,y)·disps_prior, weighted by a flat alpha (times far_gain), gated by m = disps_prior > 0, with s a 2×2 bilinear grid solved jointly with the depths (geom/ba.py:194). That interface has exactly four free parameters per keyframe, and they determine what the prior is allowed to say.

Scaling every prior disparity by a constant is absorbed exactly — s → s/c leaves rd unchanged — and so is any scale field bilinear in image position. The units a prior arrives in are therefore not a knob at all. The dumps confirm it: two priors whose raw disparities differ by 1.44× produce fitted grids that differ by the reciprocal, and the product lands on the tracker's own median.

Mean over every keyframe of each slam_depth.npz. The prior's own scale cancels against the grid it is fitted with: a “rescale the served depth” experiment cannot move anything, and never needs to be run.

Only two things about a served depth can reach the solver: its disparity shape modulo a bilinear scale field, and where that shape sits relative to zero disparity. An additive disparity offset is the one affine degree of freedom the alignment cannot absorb — which is why the pedestal knob is the only affine knob that does anything.

The anchorWhere the alignment is fitted, and where it is merely asserted

The scale parameter's Jacobian is Jso = −m·disps_prior·Jbi, so a pixel's leverage on the grid goes as its disparity squared. Binning every pixel of every keyframe by the depth the prior gives it, in multiples of that frame's median:

On the lidar-covered pixels the same weighting reads 77% under 10 m, 20% at 10–20 m, 2.3% at 20–40 m, 0.2% at 40–80 m. Turning far_gain from 1 to 2 moves the 20–40 m share from 2.3% to 3.9% — it tilts the weighting, it does not relocate it.

82% of what fits the alignment sits nearer than the frame's own median depth; 96% nearer than 1.5× it. Everything the prior says past that is an assertion, carried into the solve at full alpha and never checked by the fit that scaled it.

The errorThe trajectory error is one number, and it is not accuracy

Every arm on disk was re-measured from its raw traj_full.txt against the odometry poses: Sim(3) ATE, and the standard deviation of the local Sim(3) scale in a sliding 31-frame window — the map and trajectory breathing together. The two are the same number up to a constant.

α=0.05, far_gain 2 — 54 arms α=0.01 flat — 18 arms line: ATE = 66 × wobble

Hover a point for the arm. Excluding one blown-up arm (wobble 1.92, ATE 45.8, off scale) the correlation over the remaining 71 arms is +0.988 and the median ratio is 66 m per unit of wobble; within the α=0.01 group alone, +0.990. The wobble is a real gauge motion, not a pose artefact: per keyframe it tracks the map's own depth ratio to lidar in a fixed 8–20 m band at −0.97.

The same thing in KITTI's own terms: the mean 100 m chord ratio is 0.98–0.99 for every arm, good and bad alike. What separates them is its spread — ±0.056 for the best arm against ±0.186 for the worst. Nothing here is a bias that a better global scale would fix.

The most accurate prior on record — the LoRA-adapted VGGT, within 7% of lidar out to 40 m — tracks at 14.2 m. The least accurate, a clamped Omnidata that puts 40–80 m structure at a third of its true depth, tracks at 3.8 m. Fitting an affine relation to the lidar agrees: every prior wants a negative disparity offset (−0.21 median disparity for omni, −0.085 for VGGT base, −0.024 adapted). Every knob that helps the trajectory pushes the other way.

The familyOne transform, three sharpnesses — and one that says nothing

The two knobs in the tree are not two ideas. Served disparity q' = (qk + bk)1/k with b = median(q)/tag is a clamp at k = ∞, a pedestal at k = 1, and nothing has been run in between. All three bound the served depth at tag × the frame median; they differ only in what they charge the near field for that bound.

@ceil1.45 — k = ∞ @soft1.45 — k = 2 (untried) @ped1.45 — k = 1

Everything left of 1.0 is the half of the frame that carries the parallax and 82% of the alignment's leverage. The clamp leaves it untouched and truncates; the pedestal pays 26% of it at half the median depth; the quadrature member pays 5.5% and still saturates.

InferenceCan the tag be computed instead of swept?

Every ratio above is a value someone tried. The question is whether one could have been derived — from the prior and the tracker alone, with no lidar and no sweep. Each tag-vs-ATE curve on disk that brackets its own optimum was located by a parabola through its best three points, and nine statistics of the served distribution were read at that optimum. A statistic that lands on the same value at every curve's optimum is a rule; one that does not, is not.

Two priors, two families, three optima. The tail statistics that an earlier sweep matched on (p95, p99) vary by a third; the one that does not is a comparison against the tracker, not a property of the prior alone.

The push. Take the pixels the tracker itself calls far — its own disparity below ⅔ of its frame median — and ask how much nearer than that the aligned prior insists they are. At the optimum of every curve measured, that ratio is 1.89, 1.89, 1.95. Not a fixed tag, not a fixed tail: a fixed argument with the tracker. Both quantities are in the video buffer at run time, so the rule is computable online, with no GT and no sweep.

Where the rule breaks

It locates optima; it does not predict ATE. Fitted over all 30 frozen arms, |log(push/1.9)| explains R² 0.53 of log ATE — the best of the nine by a wide margin (the next is 0.33, and no pair of statistics passes 0.66 on 30 points). The failure is one-sided and worth stating plainly: every arm under 4.4 m sits at push 1.84–1.95, and four arms inside that same band track at 5.6–8.6 m. Hitting the push is necessary, not sufficient — the second factor is the near-field cost, which is what makes this a constrained choice: hit the push, then take the family that pays the least near field for it.

Omnidata arms VGGT base arms line: push = 1.9

The band is a gate, not a predictor: nothing good lives outside it, and not everything inside it is good.

The scene that says no

RELLIS is the useful negative. Re-measuring its 231 arms the same way, the wobble law holds with a different constant (ATE ≈ 33 × wobble, corr +0.83) — but only after windows the platform barely moved through are dropped; without that guard an off-road scene with stops reads corr −0.33, which is the metric failing, not the law. And on that scene the ceiling does nothing at all: omni 31.28, @ceil3 31.39, @ceil2 31.68, @ceil1p5 32.66 — bounding only hurts. The rule agrees before the fact: that prior already pushes 10.9 against its tracker, far past target, and no bound can lower a push. The honest caveat is that every RELLIS arm is broken (wobble 1.2 means the local scale swings ±120%), so the scene confirms the rule's verdict without being able to discriminate much else.

What a controller would need

A single keyframe's push is noisy: at @ceil1p42 the per-keyframe median is 1.79 with a 10–90 range of 1.44–2.53 and a frame-to-frame step of 0.15. So the online form is not per-frame inversion but a slow integrator — hold the median push over the last ~10 keyframes at target by nudging the tag — which also removes the counterfactual weakness of the offline inversion, since it reads the tracker it is actually steering. That is the piece of code this analysis argues for next, and it is ~30 lines against adaslam/online/prior.py's existing serving hook.

Verdict, after eleven arms. On Omnidata the rule works and the plateau confirms it: it asked for 1.55, and @soft1p45 and @soft1p55 both land at 3.89 m. On VGGT it over-asks — 1.20 for @ped against 0.99, 1.28 for @soft against 0.98 — and its sharpest cross-prior prediction is dead: base@ceil1p45 was to land far below @ceil1p5's 9.56 and landed at 9.14. Retuned on all five curves, the same statistic gets to a 7% mean tag error; what it cannot do is survive a change of scene.

The mask settles the mechanism, and not subtly. Same threshold, the same 25.9% of the frame: pinned at 1.45× the frame median it is the best arm on record (3.83 m); deleted it is worse than doing nothing at all (16.28 against 11.40 raw) — a factor of 4.3 between two readings of the same pixels. And the wobble is monotone in how tightly that far field is held: mute 0.241 > raw prior 0.168 > bounded 0.067 on Omnidata, 0.277 > 0.233 > 0.072 on VGGT. The prior's far field earns its keep as a rigid anchor, not as information — at the optimum it is asserting 0.27× the true depth out there.

The family ties on one prior and splits on the other

On Omnidata all three sharpnesses reach the same place: 3.83 (clamp, k = ∞), 3.89 (quadrature, k = 2), 3.94 (pedestal, k = 1), against a ±0.08 m floor — and their near-field costs are 0%, 11% and 29%. The hypothesis this family was built on is therefore wrong: once the bound is right, what it charges the near field does not matter on that prior.

On VGGT it matters enormously, and with the opposite sign. @ped1 3.79 (40% near cost) < @soft1 4.86 (18%) < @soft1p2 8.97 (14%) ≈ @ceil1p45 9.14 (0%). Correlating log ATE against near-field cost inside each prior: −0.71 on VGGT, +0.58 on Omnidata. The near-field compression Omnidata is indifferent to is the thing VGGT needs, and a clamp cannot supply it at any tag.

The raw mid fields say why, in the same direction the assertion table did: Omnidata already asserts 10–20 m at 0.89 of true depth, VGGT at 0.96, and a clamp leaves that untouched. A prior that is faithful in the mid field has to be compressed there too before it tracks — which is what the two families that touch the near field do, and the clamp does not.

Two questions, two statistics

The near band — pixels the tracker puts closer than half its median depth — is what says which family is even viable, and it is the one thing a clamp cannot touch at any tag. At the five optima it sits at 0.91 (spread 4%). Untransformed, Omnidata reads 0.937 and VGGT 0.965: near enough for Omnidata, out of reach for VGGT, which is exactly the 9.14-versus-3.79 the clamp and the pedestal returned on that prior. The same statistic run on the adapted VGGT reads 0.971 — so that prior, never swept, is predicted to need a pedestal too, at a tag of 1.62.

And the push doubles as the gate on whether to bound at all. Untransformed it reads 1.48 on Omnidata, 1.32 on VGGT, 1.52 on the adapted VGGT — all far under target, all needing a bound — and 8.65 on RELLIS, already past it, where no bound can help and none did.

But R is not a property of the prior. Same prior, same tracking config, frames 1000–2000 instead of 0–1000, and VGGT's pedestal optimum moves from 1.0 to 0.8: 0.7 → 4.97, 0.8 → 3.46, 0.9 → 4.02, 1.0 → 4.90, 1.1 → 6.04. A tag calibrated once per prior is already wrong one window later.

Does the statistic follow the scene? Partly.

That question was worth one extract run, and it is now answered on the second window's own dump. The rule does move — it asks 1.04 on f0–1000 and 0.65 on f1000–2000 — but it moves twice as far as the optimum does (0.99 → 0.82, a 17% shift, against the rule's 37%). Its 21% under-shoot on the new window would cost about 2 m of ATE, which is worse than simply reusing the previous window's tag.

The reason is visible in one number: the push measured at each window's own swept optimum is 1.942 on f0–1000 and 1.870 on f1000–2000. With a well this narrow — 2.5% off target roughly doubles the tag error — that 4% is the entire miss. And the two windows are otherwise indistinguishable: median lidar depth 9.7 against 9.9 m, served tail 3.51 against 3.49 median-depths, far-pixel share 30.7% against 28.5%. Nothing descriptive about the scene predicts the shift, which closes the door on a "scene statistic → R" lookup as well.

Nor does re-parameterising the transform against the prior's own tail, which is the obvious next thing to try. Expressing the pedestal's added disparity b at each window's own optimum against tail references instead of the median moves every one of them by the same 18–21%:

VerdictThe target moves, and that kills the controller

Both in-situ runs are in. Each tracked under the pedestal its own window's sweep chose, so the push on its dump is measured on the tracker the transform actually produced — no counterfactual left.

The counterfactual estimates were accurate to 0.8% and 2.8% — the offline inversion was never the error. What is not constant is the target itself: 1.93 against 1.82 on the refit alignment, 1.90 against 1.52 on the grid JDSA actually fitted.

So a controller holding 1.93 would make the same mistake. The 21% miss on the second window was not an artefact of scoring a candidate transform against the wrong tracker field; the push at the optimum genuinely differs between windows, and a fixed-target loop — online or offline — chases a number that has moved. That is worth knowing for the price of two extracts: the ~30 lines do not get written.

The other candidate has the opposite failure. The near band held tighter in situ (0.905 against 0.884), but inverting it gives 1.10 on the first window and 1.07 on the second — it is nearly blind to a shift the truth makes at 17%. One statistic over-reacts by 2×, the other does not react at all.

The shift itself is now confirmed rather than assumed: these two extracts are independent runs of the same operating points and they reproduce the sweeps — 3.64 m at @ped1 on f0–1000 (the end2end arm and its three seeds read 3.79 / 3.62 / 3.64 / 3.70) and 3.48 m at @ped0p8 on f1000–2000 (end2end 3.46). The optimum really does move between two halves of one sequence.

What the remaining uncertainty is worth

Worth keeping in proportion. Bounding the tail at all is worth 8–12 m of ATE (15.63 → 3.79 on VGGT, 11.40 → 3.83 on Omnidata). Getting the tag right within a sensible bracket is worth 1–3.5 m. The zero-run rule was chasing the second-order term, and two of its three plausible statistics are now excluded.

So the protocol that stands: let the rule place one arm, then bracket it ±25% — three arms, about 18 minutes, per new scene or prior. Against the seven-to-nine-arm sweeps that produced every curve here, that is most of the saving the rule was ever going to deliver.

Where the next metre is, if it is anywhere

Not in R. The structural comparison of the two priors turned up a confound worth more than the tag: Omnidata is fed a hard Resize((512, 512)) of an 848×256 frame (motion_filter.py:62) — a 3.3:1 image squashed to 1:1 — while VGGT gets an aspect-preserved resize. Every Omnidata-vs-VGGT comparison in this project carries that asymmetry, and it plausibly explains the mid-field compression that decides which transform family Omnidata needs. One arm tests it, but it needs a small prior class that owns its own resize rather than a spec modifier, since the squash happens inside the extractor before the model call.

MethodWhat was measured, and what it cannot say

  • Depth numbers come from the seven full/slam_depth.npz dumps (disps_prior, disps, dscales, poses) at the 1/8 grid JDSA runs on, against data/KITTI/00/depths resized nearest and sampled at the same [3::8, 3::8] offsets the tracker uses.
  • Trajectory numbers were recomputed from raw traj_full.txt files with an own Umeyama Sim(3); no results.json was read.
  • The lidar covers 56% of the frame — none of the sky, ~45 pixels per keyframe beyond 40 m. Every far-field ratio rests on those pixels; nothing here measures what the prior does above the horizon.
  • Offline transform screens (near-field cost, asserted profile, grid tilt) apply a candidate transform to a prior recorded under a different run's keyframes and tracker field. They predicted the realised grid tilt of the clamped arm to within 0.002, but they remain counterfactual.
  • Two mechanisms were tested and do not survive: per-point inter-frame repeatability of the prior (VGGT is twice as repeatable as Omnidata and tracks worse), and far-field contamination of the near-field alignment (it ranks the clamp below the raw prior, and the mask above both).
  • The optimum of each tag curve is a parabola through its best three points in log-tag, not a measured arm; the rule is read at that interpolated tag.
  • The local-scale wobble needs a motion guard — windows the platform covers less than 3 m of GT path through cannot identify a scale, and on a scene with stops they are most of them. Without it RELLIS reads corr −0.33 instead of +0.83.
  • The push inversion is counterfactual in the same way: it evaluates a candidate transform against a tracker field recorded under a different one. The arm actually run at @ceil1p5 realises the push its inversion predicts, but a family that rewrites the near field (the pedestal) is where that assumption is weakest — and that is exactly where the inversion misses.
  • Every ATE is a single draw.
